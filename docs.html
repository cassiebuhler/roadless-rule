Roadless Rule: 2026 Rescission Proposal proposed · not enacted

The datasets and methods behind the map and data assistant for the 2001 Roadless Area Conservation Rule and USDA's 2026 proposal to rescind it. The proposal is not law; the 2001 rule remains in force.

Datasets in this app

Every layer is a published government or academic dataset, converted to a cloud-native format and served as-is. Sidebar labels follow one form: what it is · PUBLISHER vintage. “STAC” opens the machine-readable metadata: schema, column descriptions, license, lineage and download URLs. These layers are here because “roadless” is not “inaccessible.” The 2001 rule prohibits road construction and reconstruction; it does not close land to the public. Roughly 135,000 miles of Forest Service trail run across the National Forest System, and the rivers layer marks reaches the Park Service inventoried for their recreational and scenic value. A map showing only roads invites the inference that a roadless area is unreachable, which the trail network contradicts.

Do not read a trail as a road, in either direction. Trails are excluded from every road-proximity figure in this app, matching 36 CFR 294.11 (“a motor vehicle travelway over 50 inches wide”). The National Trails System layer is a subset of the two agency layers, so adding the three mileages triple-counts the designated routes.

The trail schema is only partly harmonized. admin_agency and nts_designation are clean and safe to filter on. trail_type, trail_class and trail_surface are not: they carry each source agency’s raw vocabulary, so trail_type has 41 distinct values that collapse to 31 once case is folded (4WD Low, 4WD LOW, 4wd Low), and trail_surface has 62. Any count grouped on those columns will split the same category across several rows.

The rivers inventory is not a protection layer. NRI listing records that a segment was assessed and found to have outstanding values; it confers no designation. The app’s Wild & Scenic designated stratum lives under Comparison strata and comes from PAD-US — a different dataset answering a different question. Do not combine the two. Management on the NRI layer is free text (1,827 segments leave it blank, the rest name individual forests and monuments), so it cannot be filtered as an agency column.

Methods

The rulemaking

On 2026-08-20 the Forest Service proposed to remove and reserve 36 CFR part 294, subpart B — the 2001 Roadless Area Conservation Rule. This app holds the public datasets that rulemaking draws on, so its figures can be recomputed directly.

Federal Register citation91 FR 53827 (2026-08-20)
RIN0596-AD66
DocketFS-2025-0001
Notice of intent90 FR 42179 (2025-08-29)
Comment period closes2026-09-21

This is a proposal, not law. The 2001 rule remains in force, so copy here uses the conditional: the proposal would remove protections from the areas shown. Layer labels carry legal status as its own field, so PROPOSED for rescission reads as a status.

eCFR returns the wrong regulation. The printed text of 36 CFR §§ 294.10–294.18 is the 2005 State Petitions Rule. The 2001 rule, the version in effect, must be read from 66 FR 3244.

Every Draft EIS table and page cited here is traceable to a fixed copy: the Federal Register notice, DEIS Volume I and the Economic Analysis were captured on the publication date under catalog/usfs/roadless-rule-2026 in data-workflows (#589).

How it works

The map reads cloud-native files (PMTiles, Cloud-Optimized GeoTIFFs, GeoParquet) hosted on the National Research Platform directly in your browser — there is no tile server, and only the bytes for the current view are fetched. The assistant turns plain-English questions into DuckDB SQL over the same Parquet files, discovering schemas through a STAC catalog. Nothing is modeled or estimated by the app: the processing is format conversion, plus a display-time filter where a label says so.

The deployed assistant runs on the NRP open-llm-proxy, a shared university inference service using open-weight models. The full stack is open source — see the project repository and the geo-agent core library.

Asking the assistant

  • Any percentage, with its base named: “What share of rule-affected roadless area has high or very high wildfire hazard?”
  • Compare against a specific base: “What percent of Forest Service land in Montana is roadless? Say which extent layer you used.”
  • Show, hide, filter, restyle, zoom: “Make the rule-affected layer 50% transparent”, “show only fires after 2015”, “zoom to the Tongass.”
  • Export: “Give me a CSV of rule-affected roadless acreage by state.”
  • Query what isn't drawn: slope, watersheds, census geographies and drinking-water source areas have no map layer but are queryable.

Vector layers have no fixed legend — hover a feature for its attributes. Every tool call is logged in the per-turn timeline. The assistant panel resizes by dragging its edge.

Notes on specific datasets

CATEGORY is not rule status. In the roadless layer, CATEGORY (1B / 1B-1 / 1C) records forest-plan direction from before the rule took effect. 1C reads as though road construction were allowed; it was not. The 2001 rule prohibits it across every inventoried roadless area regardless of category.

Idaho and Colorado boundaries here are the superseded 2001 ones. Both states adopted their own rules (2008 and 2012) and the operative boundaries are published as separate datasets, not included here. No attribute in the data records which rule governs an area; the 58.4M / 44.7M split is a filter on STATE alone.

USFS cautions that source scales vary and that these boundaries will not align with other datasets, so treat adjacency and buffer results as approximate.

The three PAD-US strata are the deduction behind the smallest acreage base, but each is drawn for all managers, so no single layer is itself the deduction — and they can overlap each other (a wild river segment can run through wilderness), so do not add their acreages. The combined layer also stacks overlapping polygons for the same ground: unfiltered it totals 8.68B acres for a country with ~2.3B, so SUM(GIS_Acres) over it is not meaningful at national scale.

Wild segments are defined by classification, not by designation type. PAD-US files all Wild & Scenic River records under one type (910 features, 5.3M ac) regardless of class; using all of them would be 4× the acreage. Eligible and suitable segments are excluded as candidates, not designations.

PAD-US carries its own roadless records (~58.2M ac, close to the USFS 58.4M). It is a different rendering of the same inventory, so never add it to the USFS roadless layer.

Four antimeridian-wrapping units are dropped from the wilderness layer (Papahānaumokuākea, Pacific Remote Islands, Alaska Maritime NWR, Aleutian Islands WA) because their unwrapped longitudes make MapLibre draw each as a stripe across the map. All four are FWS-managed, so none is a comparison stratum for NFS roadless area, but the map understates Alaska and Pacific protected area until data-workflows#617 lands. The parquet is unaffected.

WHP measures hazard, not risk. It indexes relative potential for high-intensity fire that would be difficult to control. Its own metadata states that it is not a measure of risk to homes, communities or people, and does not account for what is exposed.

CONUS and Alaska cannot be pooled. The class breaks are percentiles computed within each domain, so “Very High” is index > 1,985 in CONUS and > 8,912 in Alaska, 4.5× apart. Use the continuous index for any CONUS-vs-Alaska comparison, and turn on both toggles for national coverage (geo-agent#349).

Alaska's high/very-high WHP acreage is zero, not missing. USFS land in Alaska is Tongass and Chugach coastal temperate rainforest, at the bottom of the Alaska hazard distribution. A national high/very-high share changes substantially depending on whether Alaska is in the denominator.

The classified 1–5 WHP product is query-only because its rasters carry no colour table upstream, so any map legend for them would be invented.

MTBS perimeters mix fire types, and the two map layers do not sum to the dataset: 4,689 Unknown records appear in neither.

MTBS severity has two hex assets and they are not interchangeable. One gives the dominant class per cell, the other each class's fractional coverage; area and share questions need the fractions. Increased Greenness, the processing mask and background are not severity levels — keep them out of any severity denominator.

Severity is not a complete annual record. Six source mosaics are permanently unavailable upstream (CONUS 2004 and 2017, Alaska 1987, 1995, 2001, 2013) and Alaska has no 2024. A missing year means not mapped, never “nothing burned”. Perimeters cover all 41 years.

Reburn makes “acres burned” ambiguous. 45,128,351 hex cells burned at least once against 57,552,314 (fire, cell) pairs — cumulative fire-years exceed unique ground by 27.5%, and one cell burned 19 times.

FPA-FOD point precision has a ~1 km floor. Many records are geolocated from PLSS descriptions, so distance bands below about a kilometre are not defensible, 0.5-mile buffers included. This is also the 7th edition, which backfills previously underrepresented states, so pre-2021 counts differ from figures quoted off the 1992–2020 edition.

The cause mix inside roadless areas differs from the national pattern. Nationally, 76.4% of ignitions are human and 13.4% natural; inside rule-affected roadless areas it is 71.6% natural against 26.4% human. State the base with either figure.

This is the SILVIS WUI, not the HFRA WUI used in the Draft EIS. The definitions differ and are not interchangeable.

The two road sources overlap, and nobody has deduplicated them. Forest Service roads frequently reappear in TIGER as vehicular trails or private service roads: in a test area on the Bitterroot National Forest, 97 of 112 RoadCore segments (87%) fell within 25 m of a TIGER line. Combine the two by distance to the nearest road across both, never by summing. TIGER also carries no length column, which is why its layers are labelled in segments and RoadCore's in miles — the units are not comparable.

Neither source alone is the Draft EIS analysis. RoadCore is Forest Service roads only; the DEIS buffered “National Forest System roads and other authorized public roads” (Vol I fn. 10), so a reproduction needs the TIGER layers too.

Which TIGER classes count as “authorized public roads” is a judgment call the agency never published. Four non-motorized classes are excluded here outright. Two more are genuinely contestable and together carry 1,436,550 segments — private service roads (logging, oil field, ranch) and internal Census use. They are inside the mapped motor-vehicle layer, and dropping them moves any proximity figure.

No dataset reproduces the regulatory definition of a road. 36 CFR 294.11 counts unclassified and temporary roads, and the Forest Service maintains no national database of temporary roads (DEIS Vol I fn. 20), so any road-proximity figure from these layers is a lower bound. A further 8,204 records holding 4,745 miles carry no geometry at all.

NLCD and LANDFIRE both stop at the CONUS border, and Alaska holds 14,778,681 roadless acres — 25% of the all-IRA total and the largest single state. Any land-cover or condition share computed from them silently drops it.

Five vegetation condition codes are not rated for departure at all: water, snow/ice, developed, barren and agriculture. They are unrated, not low-departure, and belong out of the denominator of any “share of land that is departed” statistic. Inside rule-affected roadless area they are 4.1% of cells.

Departure shares are shares of cells, not acres, because the fractional-coverage table that acre accounting requires is not published for this layer. For reference, across rule-affected roadless area 14.6% of rated land is Class III (high departure) and 33.0% is Class II.B or worse.

LANDFIRE 2024 and WHP 2023 are not one snapshot. The hazard layers are built from LANDFIRE 2020 fuels, three updates older.

Roadless acreage appears in the Draft EIS against several different bases. The largest and the smallest differ by more than 18 million acres, so every percentage depends on which one is the denominator. This ladder is DEIS Vol I Table 12 and its footnotes.

The base changes the answer. The ten Western states (AK, AZ, CA, MT, NV, NM, OR, UT, WA, WY) hold 95.61% of the rule-affected base and 73.16% of the all-IRA base — the same geography, either figure. The assistant names the base with any percentage it computes.

A percentage of Forest Service land uses a different base entirely: Forest Service surface ownership, 193,174,461 acres nationally. The proclaimed, administrative and ranger-district boundaries are not ownership — they enclose private, state and other-federal inholdings, and substituting one inflates the denominator by up to 22.6%. That is why all three are drawn as outlines with no fill. (PAD-US 4.1 fee filtered to USFS gives 193,275,732 acres, within 0.05% from an unrelated publisher.)
